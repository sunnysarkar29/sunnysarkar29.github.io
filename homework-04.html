Problem 1 — \(G_1(s)=2.5/(s^2+3s+25)\)

EXACT QUESTION TEXT FROM SUPPLIED PDF · Homework 4

Consider the following transfer function

\[G(s)=\frac{2.5}{s^2+3s+25}.\]
  1. Draw the Bode plot, the Nyquist (one-sided) plot , and the Nichols plot. The Bode plot is expected to be fairly accurate, as accurate as examples in class, while the Nyquist and Nichols plots can be more approximate.
  2. Plot the Bode plot, the Nyquist (one-sided), and the Nichols plot on the same figure using three subplots. Make sure the plots each have titles and the axes are labeled.

Tip: By default, MATLAB’s Nyquist() command makes plots using positive and negative frequencies (this will make sense later in the quarter). To plot the one-sided Nyquist (just the positive frequencies), use MATLAB’s freqresp command or you can call out the real and imaginary parts using MATLAB’s Nyquist command with [real, imag,wout] = nyquist(sys). Note that you’ll need to use squeeze or reshape on the outputs of freqresp, Bode, or Nyquist, because the output vectors have extra dimensions.

Before revealing the reasoning

Compute G(jω), separate its real/imaginary parts and magnitude/phase, then sketch all three representations.

Worked solution — answered by question part

Jump to: (1)(2)

Part (1) — Hand-sketch the Bode, one-sided Nyquist, and Nichols curves

Normalize the denominator to \(s^2+2\zeta\omega_ns+\omega_n^2\):

\[\omega_n=5,\quad\zeta=\frac3{2\cdot5}=0.3,\quad G_1(0)=0.1\;(-20\text{ dB}).\]

The complex poles are \(-1.5\pm j\sqrt{25-2.25}=-1.5\pm j4.770\). The Bode magnitude begins at \(-20\) dB, may have a resonant bump near \(\omega_r=5\sqrt{1-2(0.3)^2}\approx4.53\), then falls at \(-40\) dB/decade. The phase shifts from \(0^\circ\) toward \(-180^\circ\). On the one-sided Nyquist curve, the path starts at \(+0.1\) on the real axis and approaches the origin from the negative-real side. The Nichols plot shows the same phase/magnitude pair as a parametric curve.

Part (2) — Produce labeled MATLAB plots together in three subplots

Calculate \(G(j\omega)\) at a common grid of positive frequencies and use the same complex response for all three views. One reproducible plotting method is:

sys=tf(2.5,[1 3 25]);
w=logspace(-2,3,800); H=squeeze(freqresp(sys,w));
figure('Name','Bode / Nyquist / Nichols');
t=tiledlayout(2,3,'TileSpacing','compact');
nexttile(1); semilogx(w,20*log10(abs(H))); grid on;
xlabel('Frequency (rad/s)'); ylabel('Magnitude (dB)'); title('Bode');
nexttile(4); semilogx(w,unwrap(angle(H))*180/pi); grid on;
xlabel('Frequency (rad/s)'); ylabel('Phase (deg)');
nexttile(2,[2 1]); plot(real(H),imag(H)); axis equal; grid on;
xlabel('Real'); ylabel('Imaginary'); title('Nyquist (+ frequencies)');
nexttile(3,[2 1]); plot(unwrap(angle(H))*180/pi,20*log10(abs(H))); grid on;
xlabel('Phase (deg)'); ylabel('Gain (dB)'); title('Nichols');

The left column contains both Bode magnitude and phase, with the one-sided Nyquist and Nichols plots occupying the other two columns. This preserves the three-view comparison without omitting Bode phase.

Self-check: Do Bode, Nyquist and Nichols share G(0) and high-frequency limits?

Problem 2 — \(G_2(s)=0.6(s+30)/(s^2+4.2s+9)\)

EXACT QUESTION TEXT FROM SUPPLIED PDF · Homework 4

\[G(s)=\frac{0.6(s+30)}{s^2+4.2s+9}.\]
  1. Draw the Bode plot, the Nyquist (one-sided) plot , and the Nichols plot. The Bode plot is expected to be fairly accurate, as accurate as examples in class, while the Nyquist and Nichols plots can be more approximate.
  2. Plot the Bode plot, the Nyquist (one-sided), and the Nichols plot on the same figure using three subplots. Make sure the plots each have titles and the axes are labeled.
Before revealing the reasoning

Factor the numerator zero and underdamped poles; compare the low- and high-frequency limits before sketching.

Worked solution — answered by question part

Jump to: (1)(2)

Part (1) — Hand-sketch the Bode, one-sided Nyquist, and Nichols curves

The denominator gives \(\omega_n=3\) and \(\zeta=4.2/6=0.7\), so the resonance is weak or absent. The zero is at \(-30\) rad/s, well above the natural frequency. DC gain is \(0.6(30)/9=2\) or \(6.02\) dB. At frequencies above \(3\) rad/s but below \(30\) rad/s, magnitude falls approximately \(-40\) dB/decade; above the zero it falls \(-20\) dB/decade. Phase initially tends toward \(-180^\circ\) from the pole pair and then recovers toward \(-90^\circ\) from the zero.

Problem 3 — \(G_3(s)=200(s+10)/[s(s^2+14s+100)]\)

EXACT QUESTION TEXT FROM SUPPLIED PDF · Homework 4

Consider the following transfer function

\[G(s)=\frac{200(s+10)}{s(s^2+14s+100)}.\]
  1. Draw the Bode plot, the Nyquist (one-sided) plot , and the Nichols plot. The Bode plot is expected to be fairly accurate, as accurate as examples in class, while the Nyquist and Nichols plots can be more approximate.
  2. Plot the Bode plot, the Nyquist (one-sided), and the Nichols plot on the same figure using three subplots. Make sure the plots each have titles and the axes are labeled.
Before revealing the reasoning

The pole at zero controls low-frequency phase and slope. Include the finite zero and second-order pair when unwrapping phase.

Worked solution — answered by question part

Jump to: (1)(2)

Part (1) — Hand-sketch the Bode, one-sided Nyquist, and Nichols curves

There is an integrator at the origin, a zero at \(-10\), and complex poles with \(\omega_n=10\), \(\zeta=14/20=0.7\). At low frequency,

\[G_3(j\omega)\approx\frac{200\cdot10}{(j\omega)100}=\frac{20}{j\omega},\]

so the Bode magnitude starts with a \(-20\) dB/decade slope and phase near \(-90^\circ\). Around \(10\) rad/s the zero adds \(+20\) dB/decade, while the second-order poles subtract \(40\) dB/decade, leaving a high-frequency slope of \(-40\) dB/decade. Because of the integrator, the Nyquist curve heads to \(-j\infty\) as \(\omega\to0^+\) rather than starting at a finite DC point. This is an important plot-construction detail.

Part (2) — Produce labeled MATLAB plots together in three subplots

Problem 4 — \(G_4(s)=-200(s-10)/[s(s^2+43s+1000)]\)

EXACT QUESTION TEXT FROM SUPPLIED PDF · Homework 4

Consider the following transfer function

\[G(s)=\frac{-200(s-10)}{s(s^2+43s+1000)}.\]
  1. Draw the Bode plot, the Nyquist (one-sided) plot , and the Nichols plot. The Bode plot is expected to be fairly accurate, as accurate as examples in class, while the Nyquist and Nichols plots can be more approximate.
  2. Plot the Bode plot, the Nyquist (one-sided), and the Nichols plot on the same figure using three subplots. Make sure the plots each have titles and the axes are labeled.
Before revealing the reasoning

Inspect the real-axis zero at positive s and the negative gain sign; do not infer phase from magnitude alone.

Worked solution — answered by question part

Jump to: (1)(2)

Part (1) — Hand-sketch the Bode, one-sided Nyquist, and Nichols curves

The expression can be rewritten \(200(10-s)/[s(s^2+43s+1000)]\). The right-half-plane zero is at \(+10\) rad/s, not \(-10\); the leading negative sign makes the low-frequency effective gain positive. The second-order pole factor has \(\omega_n=\sqrt{1000}\approx31.62\) and \(\zeta=43/(2\sqrt{1000})\approx0.680\).

At small \(\omega\), \(G_4(j\omega)\sim 2/(j\omega)\), so phase starts at \(-90^\circ\). The RHP zero contributes a negative phase, \(-\tan^{-1}(\omega/10)\), instead of the positive phase that a left-half-plane zero would contribute. The pole pair supplies another phase loss approaching \(-180^\circ\). This is a nonminimum-phase system: a Bode magnitude alone cannot reveal the zero's sign.

Part (2) — Produce labeled MATLAB plots together in three subplots

Calculate \(G(j\omega)\) at a common grid of positive frequencies and use the same complex response for all three views. One reproducible plotting method is:

sys=tf(-200*[1 -10],conv([1 0],[1 43 1000]));
w=logspace(-2,3,800); H=squeeze(freqresp(sys,w));
figure('Name','Bode / Nyquist / Nichols');

xlabel('Frequency (rad/s)'); ylabel('Phase (deg)');

xlabel('Real'); ylabel('Imaginary'); title('Nyquist (+ frequencies)');
nexttile(3,[2 1]); plot(unwrap(angle(H))*180/pi,20*log10(abs(H))); grid on;

Problem 5 — PI cruise-control loop: \(d\to y\) and \(n\to y\)

EXACT QUESTION TEXT FROM SUPPLIED PDF · Homework 4

Consider the cruise control example with a PI controller as shown above, where r is the reference signal, d is disturbance signal, and n is the sensor noise signal. The goal is to understand the effect of noise and disturbance on the output.

  1. Compute the transfer function from d and n to the output y.
  2. Use Matlab to draw the Bode plot for \(K_I=4\) and \(K_p=1\).
  3. Determine the range of frequencies where the disturbance d is attenuated by factor less than \(\frac{1}{10}\). Also, do the same for noise n.
  4. Choose a value for \(K_p\) so that the maximum gain for the disturbance is smaller than \(\frac{1}{10}\). Does changing \(K_I\) effect the maximum gain significantly?
Before revealing the reasoning

Write the disturbance and noise transfer functions explicitly before estimating frequency bands from the Bode plots.

Worked solution — answered by question part

Jump to: (a)(b)(c)(d)

Part (a) — Derive disturbance-to-output and noise-to-output functions

The plant is \(G=1/(s+1)\) and the PI controller is \(C=K_p+K_I/s\). With disturbance \(d\) added at the plant input and sensor noise \(n\) subtracted through the feedback measurement,

\[y=G[C(r-y-n)+d].\]

Setting \(r=0\) and rearranging yields

\[\boxed{\frac YD=\frac{s}{s^2+(1+K_p)s+K_I}},\qquad\boxed{\frac YN=-\frac{K_ps+K_I}{s^2+(1+K_p)s+K_I}}.\]

Part (b) — Plot the responses for Kₚ = 1 and Kᵢ = 4

For \(K_p=1,K_I=4\),

\[D_y(s)=\frac{s}{s^2+2s+4},\qquad N_y(s)=-\frac{s+4}{s^2+2s+4}.\]
s=tf('s'); kp=1; ki=4; C=kp+ki/s; G=1/(s+1);
D=G/(1+C*G); N=-C*G/(1+C*G);
figure; bode(D,N); legend('Disturbance to output','Noise to output'); grid on;

Part (c) — Find both frequency bands with at least tenfold attenuation

For a sinusoid, attenuation by a factor of ten means magnitude below \(0.1\) or below \(-20\) dB, not \(-10\) dB. Solving the exact magnitude equations yields the disturbance-attenuation regions

\[\boxed{\omega<0.3925\ \text{rad/s}\quad\text{or}\quad\omega>10.1905\ \text{rad/s}.}\]

The noise response is below \(0.1\) only for \(\boxed{\omega>10.839\ \mathrm{rad/s}}\). Noise cannot be strongly attenuated near DC by this controller because \(|Y/N|(0)=1\) for \(K_I>0\).

Part (d) — Choose Kₚ to bound peak disturbance gain; explain Kᵢ

For general \(K_I>0\), find the maximum disturbance gain using \(|D_y(j\omega)|^2=\omega^2/[(K_I-\omega^2)^2+(1+K_p)^2\omega^2]\). Divide the denominator by \(\omega^2\):

\[\frac1{|D_y|^2}=\frac{(K_I-\omega^2)^2}{\omega^2}+(1+K_p)^2\ge(1+K_p)^2.\]

Equality occurs at \(\omega=\sqrt{K_I}\), so exactly

\[\boxed{\max_\omega |D_y(j\omega)|=\frac1{1+K_p}.}\]

To be strictly smaller than \(1/10\), require \(\boxed{K_p>9}\). Changing \(K_I\) does not alter this peak value; it shifts the peak frequency to \(\sqrt{K_I}\). This is stronger than merely observing plots, and it answers the final subquestion algebraically.
